The Unemployment Gap

The unemployment rate counts people without a job who looked for one in the past four weeks. It does not show how long they have been looking, how slowly employers are hiring, or what happens when unemployment benefits run out. This page tracks those numbers.

Sources and methods

Why the headline rate can stay low

  • Narrow definition. You count as unemployed (U-3) only if you had no work at all and searched in the past four weeks. Someone who stopped searching is "not in the labor force." Someone who did a few hours of paid work, including consulting or gig work, counts as employed.
  • Low hiring, low layoffs. When few people are laid off, few enter unemployment, so the rate stays flat. When employers also hire slowly, the people who are unemployed stay unemployed longer. The rate measures how many people are unemployed, not how long they stay unemployed.
  • Averages across very different job markets. The national rate combines every sector and occupation, so a downturn in a few fields barely moves it.
  • Benefit records stop when benefits end. Unemployment benefit (claims) data covers only people currently receiving benefits. When benefits end, people drop out of those numbers whether or not they found work.

Data sources and latest month available

Definitions

  • Exhaustion rate (DOL formula): people receiving their final state benefit payment over the last 12 months, divided by people receiving a first payment over the 12 months ending six months earlier. Computed here from the DOL ETA 5159 report. Computed values match DOL's published quarterly rates.
  • People starting or using up benefits: monthly first payments and final payments under regular state UI, shown as a 12-month moving average to remove seasonal swings. The table shows raw monthly counts.
  • U-6: U-3 plus people who want work but stopped looking ("marginally attached," including "discouraged workers") and people working part-time because they could not find full-time work, as a share of the labor force plus the marginally attached.
  • Recessions (gray shading): NBER business-cycle dates.

Section 5 method

The Census Survey of Income and Program Participation (SIPP) re-interviews the same people every year, asking about each month of the prior year. That lets us follow someone from the month their benefits stopped to what they were doing afterward. SIPP does not record why benefits stopped. "5 or more months of benefits" is used as a stand-in for running out, since most states pay at most 26 weeks. Results are recomputed when Census releases a new year of SIPP data (usually in summer, covering the year before last).
